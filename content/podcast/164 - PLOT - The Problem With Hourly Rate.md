# 164 - PLOT - The Problem With Hourly Rate

**Length:** 20 min (~3,000 words). Cut the debate block to get to 15.

**Belief shift**
They think: the hourly rate is the thing to get right. Get yourself to $200 an hour and you have cracked it.
Actually: the hourly rate is the ceiling, not the goal. It caps you at the speed of your own two hands, and it fines you every single time you get better at your job.
It breaks when: they work out the fastest sparky on the tools is the one taking home the least per job.

## [1:00] STORY ONE: My own hourly rate years (~4 min)

Your beats, in order. Real numbers only.

- Started the business on about **$70 an hour**. Working for builders. Time and material. Thought that was just how it was done.
- What $70 an hour actually looked like on a Friday. The van, the tax bill, the wage that never came.
- The moment you stopped charging for the clock and started charging for the job. **What was the first job you priced that way? What did you quote, how long did it take?**
- The number that proves it: a **$400 smoke alarm job that takes 30 minutes**. On hourly that is a $60 job. Same work. Same van. Same you.
- Where your profit actually went once you made the switch. **Give a real before and after figure here if you have one, even a rough monthly one. Do not guess it on air.**
- Land it: I did not get better at electrical work. I got better at how I charged for it.

> Source in the brain: Ep 150 has you on record saying "I was working for like $70 an hour, something ridiculously stupid. I was working off an hourly rate, I was working for builders." The $400 smoke alarm in 30 minutes is from the Matt Aravanis call, 8 June 2026, at the 12 minute mark.

## [8:20] STORY THREE: Rob, and the price they already agreed to (~3.5 min)

Same again, prompts not script. This one is the strongest of the three if the detail is sharp.

- Set the scene. Who is Rob, where were you, what kicked the conversation off?
- The job. **What was it, and what did it cost?**
- The culture line: the trade gets called a rip off. But the customer was told the price before a spanner was picked up, and said yes.
- Your take: nobody screams rip off at a menu. You look at $38 for a steak, you order it or you don't. The price was on the wall.
- The uncomfortable half: the only reason it feels like a rip off is that the customer is quietly dividing the price by the time. That is the hourly rate hangover. They only do it because we taught them to.
- Land it: the second you put an hourly rate on a piece of paper, you have invited the customer to audit your speed.

## [11:50] THE DEBATE - the honest case for hourly (~2.5 min)

Do not skip this. Being the bloke who admits the other side has a point is why they trust you.

- There is a real body of writing arguing hourly protects the tradesman, and it is not stupid.
- The argument in one line: fixed price puts the overrun risk on you. Time and material puts it on the client.
- Where they are right: when the cause is unknown. A fault you cannot see. A wall you have not opened. Something you have genuinely never done before. On those, a fixed price is a guess, and every hour past the guess comes out of your pocket.
- Where they are wrong: they treat every job like it is unknown. Ninety percent of residential service work is not unknown. You have done that switchboard forty times. You know exactly how long it takes. Pricing that hourly is not protecting you, it is just habit.
- The one liner: **hourly is insurance. Stop paying the premium on jobs that carry no risk.**

> Reading if you want to name sources on air, all say the same thing about efficiency being punished: Housecall Pro on flat rate vs hourly for the trades, Field Nation, Tradies Success Academy, and Rhumbix on time and materials for renovation work. Links are in the notes I have sent you.

## [14:20] THE EXEMPTIONS - where hourly is the right call (~2 min)

Say these plainly. It stops the episode being dogma.

- **Builder work and site work.** You are one line on somebody else's program. You do not control the sequence, the delays, or the bloke who has not finished before you. Charge for the time.
- **Renovations and additions.** The scope moves. Walls come down, rot turns up, the client changes their mind on Tuesday. A fixed price on a moving scope is a donation.
- **Anything behind a wall you have not opened.** Fault finding. Old wiring. Chasing an intermittent.
- **Bigger teams.** Once you have three or four blokes out, the hours are how you keep score internally. Different thing entirely to how you price the client.
- The rule: **fixed price what you can see. Charge time for what you cannot.**

---

## [16:20] THE PARADOX - the smaller you are, the more you have to charge (~2 min)

This is the bit nobody says out loud. Get the wording exactly right or it backfires.

- One man band. You bill maybe five hours in an eight hour day. The other three are driving, suppliers, quoting, admin. You carry every dollar of overhead on your own. Realistically **$30,000 to $50,000 a year just to open the doors.** All of it lands on your five hours.
- That is why a solo sparky needs to be north of $200 an hour to be genuinely profitable. It is not greed. It is the maths of carrying a whole business on one pair of hands.
- Put five blokes on. Same overheads, spread five ways. Utilisation goes up because there is an office handling the quoting. The number each person has to make to break even **drops**.
- **Careful with the wording here.** Say "the rate you need to break even drops", not "your hourly rate drops". Otherwise every listener hears "the big companies are cheaper", which is the exact myth you have spent two years killing. Big companies usually charge more, they just need less of it per head to survive.
- The one liner: **the smaller you are, the more expensive you have to be. Nobody warns you about that.**

## [18:20] THE ONE THING TO GO AND DO

"Take the five jobs you do most often. The ones you could do in your sleep. Go and look at what you actually invoiced for them over the last three months, and how long each one really took. Then put one fixed price on each of those five. Not a rate. A price. That is your Monday."
